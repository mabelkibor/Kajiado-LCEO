# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Introduction

Electrification planning has evolved beyond traditional grid expansion towards
integrated approaches combining grid extension, mini-grids and standalone
systems. This shift follows recognition that settlement heterogeneity,
infrastructure costs and financial viability are core considerations for
universal energy access (Bhattacharyya, 2012; Korkovelos, Mentis, Howells et
al., 2019). In Sub-Saharan Africa, where sparsely populated rural areas, dense
urban areas and semi-urban growth coexist, cost-effective pathways require both
spatial and economic optimisation (Szabó, Bódis, Huld & Moner-Girona, 2011). GIS
and techno-economic modelling frameworks such as OnSSET are critical tools,
enabling planners to simulate scenarios balancing technical feasibility with
affordability (Mentis, Howells, Rogner et al., 2017).

This chapter examines the theoretical underpinnings and empirical findings that
shape GIS-aided techno-economic modelling of electrification pathways. The
theoretical review covers integrated electrification planning, spatial
optimisation, techno-economic frameworks, the LCOE, and financial viability
models such as NPV and IRR (Yescombe, 2014; World Bank, 2017). The empirical
review examines applications across Sub-Saharan Africa and beyond, with
attention to Kenya's regulatory environment — the Energy Act (2019) and the
Kenya National Electrification Strategy — and to sub-national work in Narok and
Kitui (Government of Kenya, 2019; Parshall, Pillai, Mohan, Sanoh & Modi, 2009).

## 2.2 Theoretical Review

### 2.2.1 Integrated Electrification Planning, spatial heterogeneity and infrastructure allocation

IEP marks the shift from uniform grid expansion towards a diversified pathway
accounting for grid extension, mini-grids and standalone systems. Its
theoretical foundation emphasises minimum-cost optimisation while accounting for
spatial heterogeneity, recognising that settlement density and morphology
strongly determine infrastructure and resource allocation (Bhattacharyya, 2012).
Geospatial findings show IEP frameworks allow planners to allocate resources more
efficiently by matching technology choices to settlement typologies (Korkovelos
et al., 2019).

### 2.2.2 Geospatial modelling in energy access

Geospatial modelling rests on spatial optimisation theory, minimising cost while
maximising service coverage. GIS provides analytical frameworks for identifying
settlement patterns, infrastructure proximity and spatial demand distribution.
GIS-based models have mapped electrification costs across diverse settlement
types, guiding planners towards minimum-investment pathways (Szabó et al., 2011).
In Kenya, where settlement heterogeneity requires differentiated technology
choices, spatial models are particularly effective (Parshall et al., 2009).

### 2.2.3 Techno-economic modelling frameworks

Techno-economic frameworks apply least-cost optimisation to evaluate pathways.
OnSSET is a recognised methodology combining spatial and economic datasets to
simulate scenarios, weighting grid extension, mini-grids and standalone options
under different settlement typologies and demand tiers (Mentis et al., 2017). It
is applied widely in Sub-Saharan Africa at national and sub-national scales
(Korkovelos et al., 2019).

### 2.2.4 Levelized Cost of Electricity

The LCOE expresses the discounted lifetime cost of a system per unit of energy
delivered, providing a common basis for comparing technologies with different
capital and operating profiles. Its application here is stated in Equations 8–9a
of Chapter 3.

### 2.2.5 Financial viability and infrastructure investment theories

NPV, IRR and payback analysis underpin infrastructure investment appraisal
(Yescombe, 2014; World Bank, 2017). Their application from two distinct
perspectives — a utility with a regulated tariff and a social mandate, and a
private developer with a commercial hurdle rate — is what allows a single
settlement to be simultaneously a public obligation and a commercial
proposition, or neither.

## 2.4 Summary of Research Gaps

Four gaps define this study's contribution.

**Resolution.** Established applications, including OnSSET work by Mentis et al.
and Falchetta et al., operate on population rasters at roughly 1 km. Appropriate
for national trade-offs; too coarse for county procurement, and blind to
dispersed homesteads.

**Infrastructure detail.** Few studies integrate actual MV/LV network,
transformer and meter data. Proxy-based approaches — night lights, population,
generic grid proximity — systematically underestimate the unelectrified
population (Bertheau et al., 2017).

**Dispersed settlements.** Spatial clustering methods routinely discard
low-density outliers as noise. In pastoralist landscapes those outliers are a
large share of the unelectrified population, and discarding them removes the
hardest cases from the analysis precisely because they are hard.

**Dual-perspective financial appraisal.** Ronoh & WRI (2025) demonstrated that
integrating primary building footprints materially improves the accuracy of
unelectrified-settlement identification, but the work is limited to Narok County
and provides no comprehensive financial viability framework for private
developers. A framework that answers both "what does the county owe?" and "what
will a developer fund?" is absent from the reviewed literature.

This study addresses all four: building-footprint resolution, detailed
infrastructure integration, explicit retention and testing of dispersed
settlements, and a dual-perspective NPV/IRR appraisal including the household
connection fee.
